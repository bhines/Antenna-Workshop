      Limitations

      These are teaching approximations. The 3D pattern comes from a simple free-space wire simulation; a real installation will differ.

      Wavelength and resonance

      Free-space wavelength λ = c / f. A resonant conductor is usually a few percent shorter than the textbook fraction of λ because of end effects, thickness, insulation, nearby objects and any board it sits on.

      Impedance and SWR

      A 50 Ω coax system does not make every antenna 50 Ω. SWR measures mismatch on the line. Low SWR does not prove efficient radiation.

      Polarisation and pattern

      Element orientation sets polarisation. Dipoles radiate broadside; Yagis and Moxons favour one direction. Real patterns depend on the surroundings.

      Baluns and chokes

      A balanced antenna on unbalanced coax often needs a current choke. Matching transforms impedance; choking stops feedline currents. They are different jobs.

      Gain units

      dBi is relative to an isotropic radiator, dBd to a half-wave dipole. dBi ≈ dBd + 2.15. Treat any gain figure without a model or measurement as a claim.

      Measuring

      Calibrate the VNA at the measurement plane. Record resonance, R, X and SWR bandwidth, and measure again in the final mounting position.

      QWhich antennas do FPV drones use?

      An FPV quad usually carries two radio links: the control link (ELRS) and the video link. Each has its own antenna, and what matters most is matching the antenna at the drone to the one on the ground, especially the polarisation.

      LinkBandOn the droneOn the groundPolarisation
      ELRS 900868 MHz (EU), 915 MHz (FCC)T antenna (bent dipole)Dipole, T or Moxon on the TX moduleLinear
      ELRS 2.42.4 GHzSmall T or sleeve dipole, sometimes ceramicDipole or MoxonLinear
      Analog video5.8 GHzCircular omni: pagoda, lollipop, cloverleafCircular omni plus patch or helixCircular, RHCP or LHCP
      DJI O3/O4, Walksnail5.8 GHzTwo linear dipoles (stock)Linear antennas on the gogglesLinear on current stock antennas

      Polarisation rules

      • Same type at both ends: linear with linear, RHCP with RHCP.
      • Linear against circular loses about 3 dB, but no orientation is ever fully lost.
      • RHCP against LHCP loses a lot, typically 20 dB or more. This is the classic mistake with analog video.
      • Two linear antennas crossed at 90° can drop out almost completely. That is why digital systems use two antennas at different angles.

      Which designs in this app

      • ELRS 868/915 and 2.4: Half-wave dipole or Sleeve dipole. Use the FPV presets 868, 915 or 2440. The T antenna is a dipole with its arms bent out sideways.
      • ELRS TX module, directional: Moxon rectangle at 868, 915 or 2440.
      • Digital VTX (DJI, Walksnail): Sleeve dipole at 5800.
      • Goggles, directional receive: Axial-mode helix or Rectangular patch at 5800.
      • Analog VTX omnis such as pagoda, lollipop and cloverleaf are not in the app yet. Their shapes need a more detailed model than this wire solver gives with simple inputs.

      QHow is dipole antenna length calculated?

      A half-wave dipole is cut a little shorter than half a wavelength in free space. A thin wire of exactly λ/2 is slightly inductive. Capacitance at the ends and the thickness of the conductor mean it resonates when it is a little shorter, about 95–98% of λ/2. There are three common ways to get the number.

      1. The 468 rule of thumb

      L = 468 / f (feet, f in MHz) l = L / 2 (each arm)

      In metres the same rule is L = 142.65 / f. Free-space λ/2 is 491.8 / f feet, so 468 is 95.2% of it. The factor was chosen for typical thin HF wire.

      Worked example at 3.7 MHz: 468 / 3.7 = 126.49 ft. Multiply by 0.3048 to get 38.55 m in total, or 19.28 m per arm.

      2. Start from the wavelength

      λ = c / f (c = 299 792 458 m/s, f in Hz) L = 0.5 × k × λ

      This is the same idea written in metric with the shortening factor k left open, so you can choose it yourself.

      3. Thickness-corrected factor k

      k = 0.9787 − 11.86497 / (1 + (R / 0.000449)^1.7925)^0.3 R = (λ / 2) / d
      d
      conductor diameter, same unit as λ
      R
      how many diameters fit in half a wavelength

      Thin wire gives k close to 0.98. Thick rods or tubes give a lower k, so they resonate shorter. This is an empirical curve fit, not a law of physics; treat it as a better first guess.

      How this app does it

      The dipole cut list uses 0.476 λ tip to tip, with the feed gap inside that length. That is 95.2% of λ/2, the same as the 468 rule. The app does not yet use the conductor diameter. Compared with the app, the k-factor method gives up to about 3% more length for thin wire and less length for thick tubes, so cut long and trim either way.

      Building one

      • Copper wire for hanging versions, rod or tube for rigid ones.
      • An insulator at each end and a centre insulator where the coax connects.
      • 50 Ω coax with a current choke at the feed. Seal the coax end against water.
      • Two high, clear supports. Height and nearby objects change the tuning.
      • Cut a little long, measure, then trim both arms equally.

      A resonant dipole is about 73 Ω in free space, so expect an SWR around 1.5 : 1 on 50 Ω coax. That is normal.

      Formulas from Omni Calculator: Dipole Calculator. Text rewritten for this app.

      1. Pick the design and frequency. On a board antenna, set the substrate first.
      2. Cut slightly long (trim factor 1.02–1.05) where the design can be trimmed.
      3. Assemble symmetrically and secure the feed point. Keep feed leads short.
      4. Check for shorts and open circuits before connecting the analyser.
      5. Measure in the intended mounting position. Note the frequency of lowest SWR.
      6. If that frequency is low, the antenna is too long: trim. If it is high, it is too short.
      7. Change one thing at a time: trim, radial angle or tap position. Remeasure after each change.
      8. Record the final dimensions next to the calculated ones.
